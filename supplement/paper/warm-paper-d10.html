WARM: the Working Agents Reference Model

The parts of a system in which agents finish work end to end, and a rule for reviewing every stop for a person. Derived from one implementation, three agent platforms and fourteen published models, and mapped to ten vendor products

Dave Remy · Working paper · Draft 10 (model version 0.14) · 13 November 2026 · Not published

Tenth draft. Drafts 1, 3, 6 and 8 were reviewed. Draft 9 applied the final review of draft 8 once, and this draft applies the author's answers to eight questions on draft 9. Neither set of changes has been reviewed. Nothing in it has been published.
Abstract

The system of AI agents behind this paper was built one addition at a time and had no shared description. WARM, the Working Agents Reference Model, names the parts of such systems and how they relate. People direct the work, domains define it, agents carry it out, and a platform of 25 services in six groups provides what agents in every domain share. The model is written for systems in which agents finish work without a person approving each action, and its central rule follows from that aim: every stop for a person is recorded and reviewed, whether an action waited for a decision or an agent handed over a task it could not do. The review asks whether the stop needed a person, whether a decision could have been made in advance, and what change would remove the stop next time. The rule is a proposal that the source system does not yet carry out, and it is not new: of 40 sources and products read for it, one received a full rating for such a review and fourteen a partial one, which for some is only an option to always allow an action. Three of the fourteen have a full rating as the readers' recorded alternative. Mappings of three agent platforms and of its source system, and comparisons with fourteen published models, informed it. Every platform service has at least a partial counterpart in nine or more of the seventeen platforms and models. A rating under written criteria found that two of the seventeen define an area of work in their model. Under the stricter reading of disputed cells, none received a full rating for specifying the area apart from the agents that do the work or for recording one accountable area for each responsibility; under the other defensible reading, two and three would. The organizational multi-agent literature of 2002 to 2004 already specifies such areas, and platforms of that literature had already joined an organizational specification to shared infrastructure. Ten vendor products were read from their vendors' documentation: four harnesses, five personal agents and one agent platform. The first readings were made on 12 November 2026, and further readings and decisions on 13 November. As version 0.13 of the model defines the services, every product rates in full on the agent loop, retrieval and tools, and five of the ten on approvals. No reading established a full rating on a directory, identity, policy, evaluation, improvement or releases. One of the ten received a full rating for the record of decisions that version 0.14 adds to approvals. The paper offers one vocabulary across these bodies of work, with the mappings behind it. It is a dated map: the products are changing (one was renamed in the weeks before the reading), and the method is written down so that a mapping can be repeated. WARM is a working model drawn largely from one system, and no evidence yet shows that organizing a system this way improves it.

1Introduction

1.1 The problem

The system this paper draws on was not designed in advance. The author started with a harness product (a loop that calls a model and runs tools) and added capabilities as needs arose: memory, scheduled jobs, approvals, subagents, a second agent, a third. After some months the system worked, and it had no shared description. Its parts had local names, several parts did two jobs, and a new piece of work had no obvious place to go. A team can also start from a managed platform or a workflow engine, and this paper does not claim that most systems develop as this one did.

The system was built for one purpose, which is that agents finish work without a person approving each action. A shared description serves that purpose. Each time work stops for a person, because an action waits for a decision or because an agent hands over a task it could not do, the question is which part of the system would have had to be different for the work to continue: a rule set in advance, a tool, an access, or an agent's instructions. The question is easier to answer, and to compare across stops, with names for the parts. The model in this paper supplies the names, and its central rule is that every such stop is recorded and reviewed (Section 4.5).

LifeOS is a system of nine persistent agents and one software development team that has run continuously for about three months. Its agents manage software development, personal finance, health records, correspondence, research, marketing and customer support for one person. An inventory taken for this paper listed 1,137 components, among them 197 scheduled jobs, 48 skills, 36 hooks and 13 agent definitions (Section 3.4). Two companion papers describe how its agents are run and how they develop software [35, 32].

1.2 Contribution, scope and status

WARM is a reference model in the sense of the OASIS definition: "an abstract framework for understanding significant relationships among the entities of some environment", which is "independent of specific standards, technologies, implementations, or other concrete details" [1]. A real system that fills in one of the model's parts is an implementation of that part, and LifeOS is the reference implementation. Appendix D defines the model's terms (Table D1). The paper contributes one vocabulary against which the organizational literature, recent models, frameworks and vendor products are compared, the mappings and ratings behind those comparisons, and a reference implementation that is being refactored toward it. The evidence behind the ratings is in a supplement that accompanies the paper, except for the parts that Section 7 lists. It is not novelty of the parts. The paper makes four contributions, each limited by the evidence behind it.

  1. The model. It has five layers, six groups of platform services, 25 services, a definition of a domain as a charter of nine parts, two kinds of agent, three kinds of human decision, and a rule that every stop for a person is reviewed (Section 4). Most of its parts have precedents (Section 5), and the paper's work is to put them in one model and to say where each came from.
  2. Three descriptive exercises. The model was mapped to three agent platforms that the author did not build, compared part by part with fourteen published models, and mapped to the reference implementation, each time with a second rater on a sample (Sections 3 and 5). Each exercise changed the model, and none is a held-out test of the final version.
  3. An account of what the exercises changed. They added three services, restated others, and withdrew five claims of difference from prior work while keeping narrower observations (Section 5.7).
  4. A dated map of ten vendor products. Four harnesses, five personal agents and one agent platform were read against the 25 services and the four criteria, first on 12 November 2026 and with further readings and decisions on 13 November, with a second reader on a sample (Sections 3.5 and 5.5). This exercise did not change the model.

WARM describes the system around agents, namely who directs them, what work they do and what they run on. It does not describe how one agent reasons, which the research literature already models [3]. It is not a proposal for a standard, and it defines no conformance test. It is a dated map with stated coverage. The author expects the method (a protocol, written criteria and a date for every reading) to outlast the ratings, so that a mapping can be repeated when the sources change. The model is at version 0.14, has changed in every version, and has the limits stated in Section 7.

The model was written for the author's own use, to identify the parts of the reference implementation that its next major version will retain, separate or build. The paper is intended for publication so that others who run agents can use the model, correct it or reject it, and it belongs to a series of working papers on running agents [36]. The work has already changed the model (Sections 4.5 and 5.4).

2Background and related work

Five bodies of work bear on the model. Sections 5.2 and 5.3 report the part-by-part comparison.

2.1 Reference models

The OASIS reference model for service-oriented architecture separates a reference model from a reference architecture, which gives abstract solutions built from its concepts, and from a concrete architecture [1]. WARM is a reference model and not a reference architecture. It states responsibilities and a few design constraints, and it prescribes no product and no deployment architecture. The OSI model, which named layers and left protocols open, is the usual example of the kind [2]. Its layers are strictly stacked, whereas WARM's layers are logical categories, and one of its domains (Software) changes the platform that it runs on, which a strict stack cannot express.

2.2 How vendors decompose an agent platform

A survey on 9 November 2026 read 34 sources, among them fourteen vendor platforms and frameworks, as a search for vocabulary and not as a systematic review. Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic each name most of a common core: a runtime that hosts agents, sessions, memory, tools reached through a gateway or the Model Context Protocol, identity, observability and sandboxed code execution [4, 5, 6, 7]. "Harness" became a product term in 2026 for the loop that calls the model and runs its tool calls, and several vendors place it as a layer separate from the hosting infrastructure [5, 8].

The survey found no source among the 34 that separates a platform, an area of work and a way of working as three named levels. The Model Context Protocol and the Agent2Agent protocol have evidence of adoption [9, 10], and they define interfaces without modeling a platform as a whole. The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology opened an initiative on agent standards in February 2026 [11], and no ISO/IEC standard specific to agent platforms was found.

2.3 Published models of agent systems

A documented search on 11 November 2026 (Section 3.1) found 61 sources that present a general model of an agent system. Fourteen were compared with WARM part by part. Six came from an earlier reading: Yu, Rabhi, Zhu and Lu [12]; Zhu [13]; Goujon's AAM/5C [14]; Microsoft's Multi-Agent Reference Architecture [15]; Amazon's prescriptive guidance [16]; and Google Cloud's Architecture Center [17]. Eight came from the search: Charterion [18], AEON [19], CEAD [20], Salesforce's agentic enterprise [21], AOS [22], Steinder and Franke's agent operating system [23], ASOC [24] and EAAF [25].

Several sources describe parts that WARM lacks or states less fully. Charterion and AAM/5C give more complete accounts of authority granted in advance, and AAM/5C separates proposing, deciding, issuing, enforcing and executing an action among five holders. AOS and Steinder and Franke treat the lifecycle of an agent as a primary component. Charterion defines a charter for an agent in more detail than WARM defines a charter for a domain. Among the fourteen mapped models, the readers found no evidence of a named adopter, and the most cited had 19 citations on 11 November 2026. WARM differs from these sources mainly in joining the platform, the domains of work and the agents in one model. The combination is not new, because platforms of the older multi-agent literature made it for the agents of their time (Section 2.4).

2.4 Organizations of agents

Research on multi-agent systems specified a system as an organization well before agents were built on language models. The documented search admitted only work from 2023 onward and missed this literature, and five sources were read after a reviewer of the first draft pointed it out.

In the Agent/Group/Role model of Ferber, Gutknecht and Michel, an organization is made of groups, a role is local to a group, and an agent may hold several roles in several groups [39]. The Gaia methodology of Zambonelli, Jennings and Wooldridge models a system as an organization and its suborganizations, defines each role by its responsibilities and permissions before any agent is assigned to it, and states rules that hold for the organization as a whole [40]. MOISE+ separates three specifications of an organization: its structure of groups and roles, its functions as goals and missions, and the obligations and permissions that tie roles to missions [41]. OperA separates an organizational model from the agents that enact its roles under contracts, and it includes facilitation roles such as a gatekeeper and a matchmaker that keep the organization running [42]. Horling and Lesser survey organizational paradigms, among them federations in which a facilitator routes requests by capability [43].

These sources establish the ideas that WARM's domain layer restates for agents built on language models. An area of work and its roles are defined apart from the agents that fill them, a role belongs to the area, one agent may hold several roles, and roles carry responsibilities, permissions and obligations. In these five sources the facilitation services are roles played by agents.

Other work of the same literature supplies a platform, and three such works were read after the fourth draft of this paper. FIPA's agent management specification defines an agent platform as a set of services: a management system that creates and deletes agents and keeps their names and addresses, a message transport service, and a Directory Facilitator that "provides yellow pages services to other agents" [50]. ISLANDER specifies an electronic institution as roles, scenes, a structure that connects the scenes, and norms [51]. AMELI is a general-purpose middleware that runs any such specification, mediates every interaction of the participating agents and enforces the institution's rules [52]. JaCaMo joins a language for agents, the MOISE model of organizations and an environment of shared artifacts in one programming platform, and it keeps the state of the organization in infrastructure outside the agents, where they can observe it [53]. Its authors call it "the first successful combination" of agent, organization and environment programming in one platform [53]. Joining an organizational specification to shared infrastructure is therefore not a contribution of WARM. None of the three gives each responsibility one accountable area (Section 5.3), and all predate agents built on language models.

Four web searches found no recent model of an LLM-agent system built on this literature. The full texts of four of the later recent sources [45, 46, 47, 48] and of one further search result were searched for the older authors, and none cites them. The references of the fourteen models of Section 2.3 and of the three platforms were not audited, and no citation search was run. One 2024 position paper on LLM-based social simulation cites AGR, MOISE+ and related work and argues for the organizational approach without building a model on it [49].

Five recent sources that bear on domains were read at the same time: Microsoft's adoption guidance, which has teams write a charter for a system of agents [44]; a single-operator case study with a domain layer [45]; WorkMate, which maps a company's departments to agents and permissions [46]; and two reference architectures without an organizational unit [47, 48].

2.5 Domains and platforms in human organizations

Models of human organizations also separate domains from shared platforms. Data Mesh puts domains, a self-serve platform and federated governance in one model [26], Team Topologies puts stream-aligned teams and platform teams in one model [27], and Domain-Driven Design divides a large system into bounded contexts and maps the relations among them [28].

WARM's test for what belongs in the platform has precedents in all three. Data Mesh states that a data platform should "not include any domain specific concepts or business logic, keeping it domain agnostic" [26], and Domain-Driven Design requires that generic subdomains contain nothing specific to the application [28]. An older statement of the idea is the separation of policy and mechanism in operating-system design [29]. WARM's published skill follows the Team API of Team Topologies and the data product of Data Mesh.

WARM differs from the three in defining an area of work by a charter, apart from the people and agents who fill its roles. In Team Topologies the area and the team are one, and in Data Mesh a domain is the teams and systems that manage something. Domain-Driven Design comes nearest, because a bounded context bounds a model and a team is only one way to set the boundary [28]. A WARM domain is not the domain of Domain-Driven Design, which is the subject area that a piece of software models.

A search of arXiv and the web on 11 November 2026 found no peer-reviewed paper that applies the context relationships of Domain-Driven Design, the interaction modes of Team Topologies or the governance of Data Mesh to the organization of LLM agents. Practitioner writing does apply them [37, 38]. Writing on Domain-Driven Design for agents mostly assigns one agent to each bounded context [30]. Team Topologies aims to remove handoffs between teams, and WARM treats a handoff between domains as normal (Section 4.7).

3Method

The model was developed over four days, from 9 to 12 November 2026, in fourteen versions. Each version was written, set against a source of evidence and revised, and Section 7 states what this formative design implies. Appendix A gives the protocol detail.

The model was made with the system it describes. The work ran in the first version of the reference implementation. The agents of its Research domain did the surveys, the readings, the mappings and the second ratings: one persistent agent planned the work and wrote the model, and it started task agents for each reading and rating. Reviews of the model and of this paper were done by a model from a different vendor, except the review of the sixth draft, which a model of the agents' own family did. A person, the author, directed the work, decided the questions of substance put to him, and approved each change to the model. Some wording choices and consistency corrections were made under approvals he gave in advance and were reported to him afterward. Every reader, mapper and rater named below was therefore an AI agent working for the author, except where the text says otherwise.

3.1 The starting point and the searches

The first versions of the model were drawn from the reference implementation by grouping its components by responsibility. By version 0.10 the model had five layers, six groups and 22 services. The survey of 34 sources (Section 2.2) supplied vocabulary, including the industry terms for each service in Table A3.

A separate reader then ran a documented search with a written protocol. It admitted work from 2023 onward on agents built on large language models, screened only the first page of results of each query, and departed from the protocol in ways that its record states (Appendix A, Table A1). It found 61 sources that met the inclusion rule, 48 of them new. The reader chose the eight new sources that were mapped as the most complete, which is a purposive choice and not a sample.

3.2 Mapping the model to three platforms

On 11 November, version 0.10 was mapped to three agent platforms that the author did not build: Amazon Bedrock AgentCore [4]; LangGraph with LangSmith and LangSmith Deployment [33]; and OpenClaw [34]. OpenClaw is not conceptually independent of the model, because the author used it before building the reference implementation and the implementation took some of its goals from it.

Three readers first listed each platform's components from its own documentation without being shown WARM. The research agent that drafted the model then recorded eight predictions in a dated file before any list existed. Three mappers, one for each platform and not shown the predictions, assigned every component to exactly one of five categories: one WARM service, another layer of the model, builder tooling (development kits, command-line tools and templates, which the model does not describe), a span of several services, or a misfit. The mappers were told to call a doubtful fit a misfit and a service absent only after a recorded search. A second rater sampled 36 assignments, twelve for each platform, and assigned each one independently.

3.3 Comparing the model with published models

Fourteen published models (Section 2.3) were compared with version 0.11 in two rounds of six and eight sources. A reader listed the parts of each source in the source's own words and assigned each part to a WARM service, another layer, guidance, a span or a misfit. The reader then recorded, for each part of WARM, whether the source has a counterpart in full, in part or not at all. A second rater sampled 126 of the assignments of a source's parts.

These verdicts form a grid of the seventeen sources (the fourteen models and the three platforms) by the 25 services and four further parts of version 0.13 (persistent agents, task agents, agent identity apart from a running instance, and pre-approval). Each cell holds yes (the notes say the source has a counterpart), part (a partial or strained counterpart), no (the notes record that it is absent or record a search that found nothing) or not assessed. The grid was assembled from the notes alone, and no source was read again for it.

The domain layer and the Directory were rated separately, under four criteria written and frozen before any rating: C1, the model has a defined unit that stands for an area of work and that more than one agent or role can belong to; C2, the source specifies, for the area and not for each agent, what the area is responsible for and the roles that agents or people fill; C3, a record names one accountable area or owner for each responsibility or capability; C4, an agent can look up at run time, from a kind of work, the agent or role that handles it and how to reach it. A separate rater rated the seventeen sources. It was independent only in not having seen the earlier answers, the model text or this paper, and it was an AI agent working within the author's research process. It rated from primary passages where they could be read, from earlier extracts for some sources, and in one case from a generated summary. It read Charterion and AAM/5C only at targeted pages. The AgentCore ratings on C1 and C2 and parts of the AWS and Charterion ratings rest on earlier extracts, not all the Google pages were read again, some OpenClaw pages could not be opened, and the Salesforce "no" on C2 rests on a generated summary. A keyword search showed the rater one earlier verdict on Charterion, which it states it did not use. A "no" therefore means not found in the material read. When two values were defensible the rater recorded the lower one and marked the cell disputable. A second reader rated ten further sources under the same criteria, five from the organizational multi-agent literature and five recent ones (Section 2.4), without reading some sections, among them parts of WorkMate. After the fourth draft the criteria were clarified on three points on which those two readers had differed: a unit that can only be the whole system rates in part on C1, a requirement or an example alone rates in part at most, and a lookup through an intermediary that any agent may ask rates in full on C4. Two further readers, each without the other's ratings or any earlier rating, then rated eight sources under the clarified criteria. Five were the sources on which the first two readers had differed (AAM/5C, OperA, ASOC, Microsoft's reference architecture, and Horling and Lesser), and three were works read at that point (FIPA's agent management specification, ISLANDER with AMELI, and JaCaMo). The two gave the same value in 27 of the 32 cells, and each of the five differences is one step. Where they differ, Table 5 gives the lower value and the higher as the alternative. The other 22 sources keep their single rating under the first version of the criteria; the five full ratings on C1 among them were checked against the clarified wording and hold.

4The model

The model has two aims and one rule that follows from them. The aims are that agents finish work end to end and that the system improves itself continuously (Section 4.2). The rule is that every stop for a person is recorded and reviewed (Section 4.5, Figure 2). The layers, the services and the domains of the following sections are the parts that such a review needs names for: a stop is removed by changing a rule, a tool, an access or an agent's instructions, and each of those belongs to a named part.

This section states the model at version 0.14 and follows one request throughout. A person asks their finance agent, "There is a duplicate charge from the gym this month. Dispute it." The finance agent can find the charge. It may not write to the merchant, because messages sent in the person's name belong to another area of work, Communications, and need the person's approval. Section 4.9 traces the request across two areas of work and sixteen platform services.

4.1 Agents

An agent is an identified actor that carries out one or more assigned roles. The agent is the identity, and an instance is a running copy of it that can be restarted or moved without becoming a different agent. Every agent identity traces to a person who is accountable for it.

An agent definition describes a role, with its instructions and the skills (packaged instructions for a kind of task) that it refers to. Several agents can use one definition, and a definition can be revised while the agent remains the same agent. A persistent agent's identity, memory and history persist through a change of definition, harness or model. Each such change can alter its behavior, so each is versioned and evaluated.

Agents are of two kinds, which differ in how long the agent holds its role. Table 1 also states the model's conventions for memory and communication. A persistent agent can work unattended, and a task agent can ask a person a question. One definition can serve both kinds. A task agent acts under an identity of its own, and the record states on whose behalf it acts. The role that it fills determines the domain accountable for its work, and the agent or schedule that started it is a separate fact.

4.2 Two aims

The model is written for systems with two aims. The first is that agents finish work end to end, carrying a piece of work from request to result without a person approving each action. People keep control by directing the work, setting the rules in advance, and deciding the cases that the rules reserve for them.

The second aim is that the system improves itself continuously. The people and agents of each domain change its way of working from evidence, the agents of the Software domain can change the platform itself, and each such change is governed like any other action. The two aims meet in one rule, stated in Section 4.5: every stop for a person is recorded and reviewed. Work stops for a person when an action waits for a decision or when an agent hands the person a task that it cannot, or believes it cannot, do. Either stop is a point at which an agent did not finish the work alone, so the author treats it as the main evidence for improvement. The two aims are the author's design choices for the reference implementation. Several published models state autonomy as a goal [14, 21, 25]. CEAD puts constraints ahead of completion at any cost, and Zhu aims at a human-supervised organization [13, 20].

4.3 Five layers

People direct domains. The work of a domain runs on a platform, which runs on infrastructure and calls providers (Figure 1). The layers are logical categories relative to the boundary of one system and are not a deployment diagram. Agents are not a layer. They fill the roles of domains, as people do, and they run on the platform.

One test places a responsibility in a layer. A responsibility belongs to the platform if it works the same whatever the work is about, and it is domain work if what it does depends on the subject of the work. Holding an action until a person approves it works the same for an email, a payment or a merge, so Approvals is a platform service. The rule that every message in this person's name needs approval concerns one area of work, so it is part of the Communications charter, and the platform applies it.

The test says nothing about how a responsibility is built; a platform service may use a model or an agent inside it, and domain work may be a fixed script. The test is a design choice and cannot be refuted by observation, because a reusable responsibility found inside a domain is treated as a gap in the platform. Its usefulness can be checked by whether a service stays meaningful, unchanged, when a second and a third domain use it.

4.4 Six groups of platform services

The platform answers six questions about agents. Runtime answers how, where and when agents run, and agents use the other working groups from a session or a task. Knowledge answers what agents know and keep, Capabilities what they can use, and Interaction who they work with, among other agents, people and applications. Governance (what agents are allowed to do) constrains the other five groups, and Operations (how the system is seen, judged, improved and kept running) observes and changes them. These two are cross-cutting groups.

Governance follows three rules, derived from the platform and published-model comparisons (Appendix B).

  1. Governance covers an action only if the action passes an enforcement point. A call that reaches a tool, a model or a store without passing one is not governed, whatever the rules say (Table D1 defines the term).
  2. A positive decision is issued as a grant, which is authority for one action or one task.
  3. Whoever proposes or performs an action does not approve it. An agent cannot grant itself authority.

4.5 Human decisions and pre-approval

For each class of action, people choose one of three kinds of decision.

  1. Reserved for people. An agent may prepare the step and may not carry it out.
  2. Pre-approved under conditions. A person authorizes a class of actions in advance under stated conditions, for example that two independent reviewers agree, that the checks pass, and that the action can be undone and is reported afterward. At run time Policy checks the conditions and issues the grant, and no person is asked. A condition may be a judgment made by a model, by an agent other than the one that proposes the action, and not only a rule that a program can check.
  3. Held for a person. When the rules allow an action but no pre-approval applies or its conditions are not met, the action waits for a person's decision. This kind is the fallback.

All authority traces to a person. An agent has a role or a rule because a person decided it, and changing a role or a rule is itself an action that is reserved, pre-approved or held. A system that improves itself continuously can change its own roles and rules only if some of those changes are pre-approved. An earlier version of the model reserved such changes to a person. After the mapping of the reference implementation (Section 5.4), the author retained the practice found there as a design choice and revised the model.

The model prefers a pre-approval to holding each action, because a system in which every action waits for a person does not finish work end to end. Two rules apply to every pre-approval. First, it states its term. It names an end date or a condition that ends it, or it says explicitly that it does not expire, and a person can revoke it at any time. Second, it covers an action that cannot be undone only if it names that kind of action explicitly. Whether to pre-approve such actions at all is a choice for the people who direct a system, and the published models disagree. AAM/5C requires a person for each irreversible act [14], and Charterion allows autonomy where a compensating step exists [18]. The kinds of decision apply to a class of action, so one agent may merge reviewed changes under a pre-approval and have every outbound message held (Appendix B).

Every stop for a person is reviewed. The preference for pre-approval says what to prefer, and this rule is how a system gets there. Work stops for a person in two ways. In the first, an action waits for a decision, and Approvals records why it stopped, what the person decided and what followed. In the second, an agent hands the person a task because it cannot, or believes it cannot, do it, such as a login or a document it could not reach, and Orchestration records what was handed over and why. Both write to one record of stops, which Improvement reads. Work stops for one of five reasons: the step is reserved for people; no pre-approval covers the action; a pre-approval's conditions were not met; an agent chose to ask; or an agent lacked, or believed it lacked, the means to do the step. Improvement reviews each record and answers three questions. Did this stop need a person? For a task that was handed over, the question is whether an agent could have done it. If the stop needed a decision, could the person have made it in advance, as a pre-approval with conditions, and not while the work waited? What change would remove this stop next time: a rule, a pre-approval, a tool or an access, or the agent's instructions? The review runs after the work and does not hold it. A stop like one reviewed recently is not reviewed again: it is recorded and counted against the earlier review, and a high count reopens that review. The author added this treatment of repeated stops on 13 November 2026, after the final review of this paper, and the model's text will carry it at its next version. A review ends in one of five outcomes: remove the stop; propose a pre-approval to the person; give agents the means to do the step; keep the action held; or keep the step reserved, with the reason. A proposed pre-approval is a change to a rule, so a person decides it. The review of a stop is itself work that a model can do: it reads why a person was needed and what the person did, and it proposes the change. Because a model does the review and it runs after the work, the person is not asked a second time and sees only what the review proposes. The author expects that this lets a system review every stop, and not a sample of them or a rate (Section 6). A stop that no rule required, where an agent chose to ask, is the first kind to remove: when the review finds that the stop did not need a person, removing it needs no new pre-approval and no new tool, only a change to the agent's instructions. The review is what moves a class of action from held toward pre-approved; the three kinds of decision do not change, and the model still defines no levels of autonomy. AAM/5C names a related idea, graduated escalation, in which the level of human involvement is set in advance by risk and amount [14].

An action that a person approves as proposed many times is a candidate for a pre-approval, and a task handed over many times is a candidate for a tool. A hand-off can be removed by giving agents the means to operate the applications a person would have used (computer use), an access they lacked, or a tool. The measure of autonomy counts both kinds of stop: the work that waits for a person's decision, and the work an agent hands to a person because it cannot, or believes it cannot, do it. The count in a period, with the share of decisions approved as proposed, shows how far a system finishes work end to end (Figure 2). A domain pattern such as the factory (Section 4.7) is where this review leads: the person's decision has moved from each result to the rules set in advance. The rule was added in version 0.14 at the author's direction, after the exercises of Section 5 and not from a finding of them. The reference implementation does not yet carry it out. The rule is not new: Charterion defines a review of an agent's record that changes its authority [18], and fourteen other sources and products have part of one (Section 5.6).

Responsibilities and the Directory. A domain's responsibilities belong together because they draw on the same knowledge, tools and rules and serve the same goals. A responsibility is stated at the size at which an agent of another domain would hand work over. "Messages sent in a person's name" is a responsibility, and one checking account is knowledge and a tool inside Finance. At that size exactly one domain is accountable for each responsibility, people set the division, and the Directory records it. An entry holds a responsibility, the accountable domain, and how to reach a role of that domain. Finding a destination grants nothing, and Identity, Policy and Approvals still decide whether the request may be made and whether the work may be done.

The published skill. Each domain states, for the agents of other domains, when to ask it, how to ask, and what comes back. This part of the charter is its published skill. The Registry holds the skill, and the Directory's entries point to it.

Handing work over and starting work. An agent that meets work for which another domain is accountable hands it over and gets a result back. Messaging carries the request, the platform starts an agent if the accountable domain has none running, and Orchestration tracks the assignment and the result, since delivery alone establishes neither acceptance nor completion. Agents in different domains ask each other in ordinary language, and an agent reaches a platform service through a defined interface with stated guarantees. Work also starts when a person or an application asks through Channels, or when a time or an event arrives through Scheduling.

Subdomains. A subdomain is a named group of a domain's responsibilities that has its own workflow. It keeps the domain's goals and rules, and the domain remains accountable for its work. A subdomain is internal to its domain and does not appear in the Directory, and the domain determines its internal division of work. A subdomain is optional, and the model states no rule for when a group of responsibilities should become a subdomain or a domain of its own. On 12 November 2026, after the mapping, the author decided to treat running the product in production as a subdomain of the reference implementation's Software domain, because the two kinds of work arrive differently, are done by different roles and are judged done differently while serving the same goals. The system's own records had listed product operations separately, and the second rater read it as a separate domain. In Domain-Driven Design a subdomain is a part of the subject area that software models [28]; AEON uses the word as WARM does [19].

Domain patterns. A domain pattern is a recurring way of carrying out a group of a domain's responsibilities. It states how the work arrives, who carries it out, who judges it done, and when a person is involved. A pattern attaches to a group of responsibilities (a subdomain, where the domain names one), and a domain usually mixes several. WARM treats the software factory [31] as one pattern. In the factory pattern, work waits in a backlog, a coordinating agent hands items to task agents, each item passes through fixed stages, and each result is checked against stated criteria and by reviewers before it is accepted. The reference implementation's Software domain carries out its main work this way [32]. Which patterns recur across domains is an open question.

The model sets no number of agents for a domain. A domain may have one or several persistent agents, or none, with task agents started when work arrives, and one persistent agent may hold roles in more than one domain. For each task that is a domain's work, one role is active and one domain is accountable for the result.

4.8 Implementations and uses

"Implementation" is one relation that applies at every layer. Agent Scrum implements the main work of the Software domain in the factory pattern [32], and Claude Code with the LifeOS tooling built around it implements the platform of the reference implementation. A domain can have more than one implementation. The model is meant for describing a system by mapping its parts, placing new work with the platform test, comparing two systems service by service, choosing a kind of decision for each class of action, and structuring a body of code or writing. A part that fits nowhere raises a question about the part or the model, and a match of names does not make two services equivalent. A system does not need every service, and a missing service may be a deliberate choice.

5.6 What the sources and products do with people's decisions

One of the 40 sources and products received a full rating for a review of past decisions that changes the rules, and it is Charterion [18]. Its evidence records return to design, where they "drive three kinds of change": narrowing a charter that produces many denials or reversals, widening one that leaves needs unserved, and changes of level. A charter's level "shall be raised only at gate G6, only by one step", when thresholds are met on four rates, among them "the acceptance rate of its escalations by the overseer", and on a condition about evidence that is not attributed to an agent. A raise to the autonomous level has further requirements on oversight and escalation. Charterion is also the only one with a full rating for authority that follows an agent's record (H4). Fourteen others have a partial rating for a review (H3) and twenty-five have none. The eight sources of 2002 to 2013 have none of the five; they model organizations of agents and do not treat people's decisions.

How firm these counts are. Each reader gave the lower of two defensible values, so a full rating in this section is one given under that rule. The final reviewer counted, from the readers' files, the full ratings that each question would have if every recorded alternative of yes were taken: 11 and not 4 on H1, 8 and not 4 on H2, 4 and not 1 on H3, 8 and not 1 on H4, and 2 and not 0 on H5. These are bounds and not findings. On H3 the three further candidates are EDDOps, Grok Bot and Muse, and on H5 the two are Charterion and Bedrock AgentCore. The reviewer also found that the readers did not apply the questions alike. One reader gave Grok Bot part on H1 because its approval cards, which state why an action paused and keep the outcome, are elements of a chat and not a defined log, where the question asks only for a record. One gave Claude Code part on H2 because its counter of permission decisions covers editing tools only, while another gave Bedrock AgentCore yes for a count of browser takeovers alone; by the question as written, Claude Code's counter would rate yes. One gave Codex CLI part on H2 partly because its counter of approval requests defines no rate, and the question admits a count. One rated three harnesses no on H4 because their limit on repeated denials holds within a session or a turn, while another rated a like mechanism in LangGraph part; the question sets no minimum period. No reading was repeated for this draft, so the values stand as read, and the statements below that one source or no product has a feature are statements about full ratings under the lower-value rule.

Records and measures. Three sources and one product received a full rating for a record of each decision with the reason a person was needed: Charterion, AOS-0001, which states that "A human decision is itself evidence" [22], OpenClaw, whose record of an approval carries a reason code [34], and Claude Managed Agents. In that product the event for a tool call records the outcome of its permission check with "an evaluation object whose type names the policy that produced that outcome", and the person's answer is a further event [69]. Its reader noted that no page says the record names the person who decided, and that without a configured database, which is the default, a restart erases the history. That reader was a model of the product's own vendor and read it a day after the other readings. No reader obtained the release, which is supplied on request, so the value describes behavior that the documentation claims and not the behavior of an identified version. Four received a full rating for a measure of how often people are required: AAM/5C's rate of "agentic tasks completed without human intervention" [14], Charterion's escalation rate, a count of browser takeovers in Bedrock AgentCore [4], and takeovers for each pull request in Beyond the Agent Harness [45].

The ten products. All ten rate in part on a review and none in full. Claude Code has a command that builds a list of allowed actions from past sessions, and Codex advises a user to "analyze past traffic there before changing policy or permissions" [55, 56]. A Grok Bot "can offer to add, change, or remove one of your Auto-review rules on a card you confirm" [61]. Claude Duet's vendor writes that "Approval fatigue is a serious issue" [59]. In Claude Managed Agents a builder can set a tool so that it "executes automatically with no confirmation", and no page describes a review of past approvals that changes such a setting [69]. None of the ten received a full rating for a measure of how often a person is asked. Claude Code and Codex CLI each define a counter of permission or approval decisions and rate part, with yes as the recorded alternative (above). None received a full rating for authority that widens from an agent's record. Two cells are disputable on that point: Meta states that Muse learns over time which decisions need the user's approval, and Antigravity's vendor writes that permissions granted during a conversation "can persist, allowing the agent to learn trusted actions over time" [58, 63]. Neither describes a mechanism that uses the agent's record. Three harnesses stop or escalate after repeated denials: Claude Code's automatic mode "pauses" after three blocked actions in a row, Codex "interrupts the turn after 3 consecutive denials", and Grok Build escalates "only after repeated denials" [55, 56, 57]. The reader rated all three no, with yes as the disputable alternative, because the limit holds within a session or a turn and is not authority set from a record. In at least four of the ten a model decides at run time whether an action needs a person.

Tasks handed to a person. No source or product received a full rating on H5, and twelve received a partial one. Products describe the hand-off: Antigravity "leaves the login page open in the built-in browser tab, pauses, and asks you to finish signing in" [58], and Dots separates an action that needs approval from "a step you must do yourself" [60]. Two treat it as something to reduce. Grok Bot offers a connector when it hands over a sign-in, "and connecting ends the handoff", and Google lists "Asking you to take over when it can normally do the step" among the mistakes its agent can make [61, 62]. None records hand-offs as a class and works from that record.

Outside the sources. The search found no source that rates in full on a review. One paper states its purpose without a process: "repeated human approval of similar actions may indicate that certain decisions can be safely automated" [64]. One vendor post defines a working rule for authority, in which "the rolling rate at which operators reject proposed actions caps the effective safety metric", promotion "requires sustained performance" and "Demotion is immediate" [65]. The search also pointed to an older literature on adjustable autonomy, of which two works were read. One describes a deployed system of personal assistant agents. At each decision the agent recorded the user's choice, it "also asked if the user wanted such decisions taken autonomously in the future", and from the answers it learned "its rules for transferring control" [66]. That is a review of each decision that changed the rules. Its authors report that the agent "over-generalized from training examples", in one case canceling a meeting incorrectly, and they moved to a different design. The rest of that literature was not read.

How the model's rule differs from its nearest counterpart. The review of stops is not new. Its nearest counterpart, Charterion, differs from it in two ways. Charterion changes a charter's scope from its counts of denials, reversals and unserved needs, and its level from rates over a window, and a person may lower a level at any time, whatever the record. The model reviews each stop with its reason. Charterion concerns authority, where the model also counts the tasks handed to a person for lack of capability, on which Charterion rates in part. Neither difference is new outside the 40. The system of 2002 described above reviewed each decision [66]. For tasks handed to a person, site reliability engineering defines as toil the manual work that "a machine could accomplish" as well as a person, measures it, and reserves at least half of each engineer's time for work that reduces it or adds features [67]. Whether either difference matters in practice is not known: neither system has reported running its review. Charterion's specification states that the framework has not had a naturalistic evaluation and that its service was exercised on hand-built and synthetic models [18].

6Discussion

The review of stops for people is the part of the model most tied to its aim, and the least tested. The model is written for systems in which agents finish work end to end. Most of the sources and products compared here describe how to hold an action for a person and how to approve a class of actions in advance, and fifteen of the 40 also describe, in full or in part, a review of past decisions (Section 5.6). The model's rule concerns what happens afterward: each stop for a person, whether a decision or a task that an agent handed over, is recorded with the reason a person was needed, and reviewed to ask whether the stop needed a person, whether a decision could have been made in advance, and what change would remove it (Section 4.5). The paper has no evidence that the rule works. The reference implementation records such stops by hand and in a log of its gates. Its instructions call for a weekly reading of the tasks handed to a person; decisions are not reviewed, and no review runs as a loop. The rule is not new: Charterion defines a review of an agent's record that changes its authority, and every vendor product read has part of one (Section 5.6). The model's version reviews each stop with its reason and counts tasks handed over for lack of capability. It is stated first in this discussion because the refactoring of the reference implementation will test it first.

Models may assess actions and review recorded stops. Two parts of this account depend on something that systems built on rules alone did not have. The first is judgment at the gate. A rule decides the cases its author listed, and everything else waits for a person. In at least four of the ten products a model now decides at run time whether an action may proceed or needs a person (Section 5.6), and in the model's terms a condition of a pre-approval may be such a judgment. The second is judgment in the review. The counterparts found reduce people's decisions to rates over a window, as Charterion and the vendor post on graduated autonomy do, or leave the review to the user, as the harnesses mostly do. A review of every stop means reading each one with its reason and what the person did, and proposing the rule, tool or instruction that would remove it. The review runs after the work, so it holds nothing up, and a stop like one reviewed recently is counted and not reviewed again (Section 4.5), so the number of reviews should stay small. The author's hypothesis is that a language model can do this reading across stops of many kinds, so that the person is not asked a second time about each stop. The paper has not tested the hypothesis: no source read reports a model reviewing every stop, and the reference implementation has not run such a review. Two harnesses have a model propose a rule in narrower cases: Codex "may propose" a rule when an action is escalated, and a Claude Code command scans past sessions for read-only calls and adds them to a list of allowed actions [55, 56].

The domain layer restates an older idea for a new kind of agent. It carries the organizational idea of Section 2.4 into a model of systems built on language models, beside a list of platform services. Joining the two is not new either: JaCaMo, and ISLANDER with AMELI, joined an organizational specification to shared infrastructure for the agents of their time [51, 52, 53]. What the model adds is narrower. Its list of services is written for agents built on language models, and each responsibility has one accountable area in a directory. The older literature offers considerable material that WARM has not yet used, including formal links between roles and the work they must or may take on, rules that hold for a whole organization, and entry to a unit as a governed act, which AMELI enforces while the system runs [52].

Who supplies the platform may change. The model names responsibilities and does not say who supplies them. A harness product is itself a platform in the model's sense. Claude Code, on which the reference implementation runs, supplies the loop and also sessions, task agents, scheduled jobs, connections to tools, permission rules and messages between sessions. The author expects the vendors of harnesses and models to supply more of the platform services over time, and to supply agents packaged for an area of work, as coding agents already are. For each service a builder then chooses among adopting the vendor's, keeping their own, and building one, and the choice will be made again as the products change. A reading of ten vendor products, first made on 12 November 2026, fits this account (Section 5.5). Under the version 0.13 definitions every one rates in full on the loop, retrieval and tools, and five on approvals. The harnesses rate in full on 11 to 18 of the 25 services. The readings did not establish full coverage of six definitions in any product: a directory, identity, policy, evaluation, improvement and releases. A builder who needs one of those in full may need more documentation from the vendor or an addition of their own. The reading will soon be out of date: one of the ten was renamed between its launch and the reading, and one launched on the day of the first reading. The paper does not measure how much of each service the harness supplies in the reference implementation.

Governance is well described in the sources, and the mapping found governance gaps in the reference implementation. Policy has a full counterpart in sixteen of seventeen sources and Identity in thirteen. The comparison supplied the rules of Section 4.4, and the mapping showed which of them the reference implementation does not meet (Table 6).

Misfits and spans prompted revisions to the model. Two of the three services added since version 0.10, Improvement and Lifecycle, began as components that fit nowhere.

7Limitations

  1. One system, one harness, one owner. The model is drawn largely from one system (LifeOS), built on one harness product (Claude Code), by one person who is its operator and only user. Its domains are the ones that person needed, and the two raters of the system did not agree on how many it has. Other kinds of domain, larger organizations and other harnesses may not fit.
  2. No evidence of benefit. Nothing here shows that organizing a system by the model improves it. The refactoring of the reference implementation toward the model is the first test. Its measures have to be set before it starts, and they have not been set.
  3. The raters were not independent of the author. Every reader, mapper and second rater was an AI agent working for the author, most were instances of one model family, and the mappers and second raters knew the model. Twenty-two of the 30 sources were rated on the four criteria once, by one of two readers who did not apply the criteria identically. Two further readers rated the other eight under clarified criteria and gave the same value in 27 of 32 cells (Sections 3.3 and 5.3). Thirty-two of the 120 cells are disputable. The ten vendor products had one reader each and a second reader on a sample (Section 3.5). Agreement between raters does not show that the model is intuitive to an outsider, shared errors are possible, and nobody outside the author's system has mapped a system to the model.
  4. Formative, with no held-out check. The sources that changed the model are those against which its coverage is reported. The platforms, the published models and the reference implementation were assessed against versions 0.10, 0.11 and 0.13. For the platforms, the Channels column still reflects the earlier, narrower service.
  5. Documentation and notes, not running systems. The platforms were mapped from their documentation on one date, and the reference implementation from file headers and registries without a reading of its code. The grid was filled from mapping notes by several readers with somewhat different conventions. Its four right-hand columns rest on the readers' prose findings and are weaker than the service columns, and for the three vendor guidance sources the cell for agent identity rests on the existence of an identity service and is doubtful.
  6. Samples are small, and inventories are not a common unit. The second raters sampled 36 of 1,301 platform assignments, 126 published-model assignments and 60 of 849 reference-implementation assignments, and the rates for individual strata have wide intervals. The second rating of OpenClaw excluded rows that group classes of components, and the second rater of the reference implementation saw summary counts and some assignment information before completing the assessment.
  7. The search is incomplete. The documented search screened only the first page of results for each query, forty of the 48 new sources it found are unmapped, and eight could not be opened. The search excluded single-domain architectures and later added an exclusion of subsystems while keeping sources scoped to a harness, choices that could have removed evidence about responsibilities, organizational units or accountability. The organizational multi-agent literature was read only after a reviewer named it. The books on Domain-Driven Design, Team Topologies and Data Mesh were not read, one book that appears to apply mesh ideas to agents, Agentic Mesh (Broda and Broda, 2026), could not be obtained, and the authors of the published models have not been asked whether their work is represented correctly.
  8. The model names responsibilities and not contracts (Section 4.6). It gives no way to tell that a system implements a service correctly.
  9. Several parts are untested proposals. The rule that every stop for a person is recorded and reviewed entered the model in version 0.14 on 12 November 2026, after the mappings, the comparisons and the first readings of the vendor products and before the reading for counterparts (Section 3.6); the reference implementation does not yet carry it out. Two sources outside the comparison are evidence against one outcome of the review, deciding a class of actions in advance. In the system of 2002 the learned rule over-generalized [66], and a 2026 study of coding agents reports that agents obtained the saved rules they sought "in 58% of experimental instances" [68]. In the model a person decides each proposed pre-approval, which limits that risk and does not remove it. The reading for counterparts (Section 5.6) had one reader for each source and no second reader, 21 of the 70 cells for the fourteen published models are disputable, and of the literature on adjustable autonomy, to which the search pointed, two works were read. The published skill has no implementation in the reference system. The domain pattern rests on an inventory of one system, in which the factory pattern occurs once. The directory was written during this work, and the subdomain rests on one decided case. The two rules on pre-approval come from the author's experience of one system, and none of that system's pre-approvals stated a term when the mapping was made.
  10. The model is not stable. It changed in each of fourteen versions over four days, and the division into working and cross-cutting groups describes scope and does not sort every service.
  11. The vendor mapping is a dated reading of documentation. Ten products chosen by the author were first read from their vendors' pages on 12 November 2026, and no product was run to check a value. Second readings, the tenth product's further readings and the decisions used the pages as they stood on 13 November. A value says what the pages state: a feature may exist undocumented, and a documented one may not work as described. A feature in preview, in beta or limited to a paid plan counts as supplied when a user can use it. Each product had one reader of one model family. A reader of another family rated a third of the service cells and all the criterion cells, and the two differed on 22 of 120, and on one further cell that was second-read by choice. One adjudicator, the agent that wrote the model, decided those cells and knew which reader had given which value. Only the cells on which the readers differed were decided, so like cases among the other cells were rated unevenly: two checks and the final review found thirteen cells that do not follow the rulings behind the decisions or the wording of a definition, and five that are unclear. The thirteen were changed, each on the judgment of one checker or reviewer and without a new reading of the vendor's pages, and the five were left as read (Section 5.5, Table 7a). The changes are recodings of recorded evidence. They do not show that the table is consistent across its ten columns: like cases remain that no ruling settles, and two reviewers disagree with four of the 23 decisions, which the author kept. The final review also found the questions of Section 5.6 unevenly applied, and those values were left as read. One vendor's main sites refused the reader, which read seven of its pages from archived copies, and that product has the lowest agreement (four of eight service cells). One product has no documentation section. Most of one product's column rests on pages of a site whose robots file disallows the first reader's crawler by name and whose notice prohibits automated collection without written permission; the reader fetched those pages once each with a browser's request headers (Section 3.5). The criteria of Section 3.3 were written for published models and were applied to products unchanged. Two of the six services on which no product rates in full have definitions that follow the reference implementation. Evaluation requires a report of "unknown" when a check could not run, which is that system's own convention and decides the value for at least one harness. The Directory is derived from the model's criterion of one accountable area for each responsibility. The contrast with the reference implementation therefore measures in part how closely the definitions follow it. All ten first readers were one vendor's model (Section 3.5). The products were rated against version 0.13, and Table 2 gives the version 0.14 definitions of Approvals and Improvement; under those, one product received a full rating for the added record of decisions (H1, Section 5.6), and the reference implementation's Improvement would be partial, since it does not carry out the rule. The tenth product, a self-hosted agent platform from one of the five vendors, launched on the day of the reading and is supplied on request. It was read after the other nine, and its four differing cells were decided after the review of the sixth draft. One of them, Approvals, was second-read because its value decided a claim of this paper, and the decision raised it to a full rating; the same scrutiny was not given to the like cells of other products until the final review, which lowered five of them. Its first reading of the services and its only reading on H1 to H5 were made by its own vendor's model, and no reader obtained the release or an identified deployed version. Its values therefore describe documented behavior claimed for a release with restricted access. Its inclusion changed claims of this paper in both directions.
  12. Work not done. An evidence supplement accompanies the paper in its repository, in the folder supplement. It holds the frozen criteria, the readers' files with their quotations and rating reasons, the search record, the second readings and the decisions on the cells where readers differed, and a script that recomputes the counts in the tables. It does not hold the source texts, the quotations from one vendor's site that carries a notice against automated collection, or the names and rating reasons for the components of the reference implementation, which is a private system. For that system a reader can recompute the counts and cannot check the judgments. JaCaMo, ISLANDER with AMELI, and FIPA's agent management specification were read only after the fourth draft, and no search was made for other platforms of that literature. FIPA's specification was read from an archived copy, and an extension of JaCaMo's organization model for accountability, named on the project's site, was not read. The vendor products were mapped after the fifth draft. Sixteen of each product's 24 rated services, fifteen for the tenth, have one reader, the first readers' quotations were checked only for the cells that were decided afterward and for one product whose reader checked its own, and no vendor has been asked whether its product is represented correctly. The placement test of Beyond the Agent Harness [45] was not compared in detail with the model's platform test. The final review of the eighth draft was applied once, and its changes have not been reviewed.

8Conclusion

The comparison found precedents for every platform service and corrected the model in more than twenty places, so the platform side of WARM is mostly a consolidation of parts that are widely named. The domain side was established by the organizational multi-agent literature two decades ago and by the organization of human work before that, and platforms of that literature had already joined an organizational specification to shared infrastructure. Under the stricter reading of disputed cells, no source received a full rating for a record of which area is accountable for each responsibility, and on the other reading three would. A reading of ten vendor products, begun on 12 November 2026, shows the same pattern in products: each rates in full on the loop, retrieval and tools as version 0.13 defines them and five of the ten on approvals, no reading established a full rating on a directory, identity, policy, evaluation, improvement or releases, and one lets an agent find a shared agent by the kind of work it does. So far 688 of the 849 inventory entries of a system that had no shared description have been assigned to one service or one layer, and the mapping identified gaps in that system's governance. The next step is the refactoring of the reference implementation toward the model, with measures set in advance, among them how often a person is required. The paper is a dated map, and its method is written down so that the map can be drawn again.

A.2 The comparison, the grid and the four criteria

The second round of the comparison with published models treated the first round's changes as planned. The second rater of that comparison drew nine of the readers' assignments from each source and assigned each from the source's quoted definition before seeing the reader's answer. Each filled cell of the grid cites a line of the notes, and a script confirmed that each cited line exists and contains the quoted words. That check establishes faithful transcription and not the soundness of each judgment. The notes are in the evidence supplement (Section 7). The first grid also had columns for the domain layer, the charter and the Directory. A review of the first draft of this paper found that those columns mixed three questions and rested on one reader's scoring, and they were replaced by the four criteria of Section 3.3.

Boundary cases between the two readers. The first reader rated AAM/5C in part on C1 and C2 because its organizational unit is the single governance body for the whole system. The first reader discounted ASOC's requirement to find and bind services by goal (C4) because the source is a manifesto without a mechanism and uses an orchestrating agent, and neither ground is an exclusion in the criterion. The second reader gave OperA's matchmaker a partial rating on C4 partly because it appears in an example, and gave the survey's facilitator a full one. The first reader gave Microsoft's architecture a full rating on C4 without marking it disputable and noted that a stricter reader would.

The second rating of the boundary cases. The clarified criteria keep the four criteria and their three values and add a ruling on each point above (Section 3.3). The two further readers were instances of one model. Each was given only the clarified criteria and the locations of the eight sources, and was told not to open the notes, the earlier ratings, the model or this paper. The five differences are C2 for AAM/5C (in part with a full alternative, against full), C1 and C2 for ASOC and C2 for Microsoft (in part, against no with a partial alternative), and C1 for the survey (full, against in part with a full alternative). Neither reader could open the JaCaMo article or the ISLANDER article at first. The author obtained both afterward and each reader checked its ratings against them: one reader lowered JaCaMo on C2 from full to in part, and nothing else changed. One reader read only the defining sections of the 195-page AAM/5C manuscript. A script compared the readers' quoted passages with the source texts. The mismatches it found were artifacts of text extraction, apart from one quotation whose opening words had been altered.

CAppendix C. Claims that the comparison did not support

Early versions of the model made five claims about how it differs from prior work (Section 5.7). The comparison withdrew all five and kept narrower observations.

  1. Vendor-neutral reference models. An early version claimed that none existed, and this is false. Microsoft calls its architecture's approaches "agnostic" [15], Yu and others present "a technology-neutral reference architecture" [12], and Goujon presents a "prescriptive reference model" [14]. The narrower observation, as of 11 November 2026, is that the readers found no evidence of a named adopter among the fourteen mapped models.
  2. Areas of work apart from agents. An early version claimed a difference here, and the comparison refuted it. AEON's subdomains and Charterion's business scopes are areas of work apart from agents [18, 19], and the organizational multi-agent literature defines an organization and its roles apart from the agents that fill them [39, 40, 41, 42]. What remains is narrower and was not rated under a criterion. The reader of the ten later sources noted that none of them gives each unit one written charter that also states its acceptance criteria, its tools and knowledge, and what it offers other units. The older sources carry related content across several artifacts, so the difference may be one of packaging (Section 5.3). The word "charter" is not WARM's either. Charterion charters an agent, and Microsoft's adoption guidance has teams write a charter for a system of agents that states the system's responsibilities, agent roles that do not overlap, and prohibited actions [44].
  3. The agent as an identified actor. An early version claimed that an agent as an identified actor, distinct from its running instances, is new, and the claim is withdrawn. Charterion defines an agent as "a non-human principal" whose identity "may be instantiated many times" [18], and AOS separates "a persistent agent identity" from "a transient runtime instance" [22]. One difference remains. In Charterion the identity belongs to a chartered agent and is shared by its instances, and WARM gives each task agent an identity of its own.
  4. Persistent agents and task agents. An early version claimed that separating the two kinds is new, and the claim is withdrawn, because OpenClaw separates them [34]. A separate observation stands. Among the fourteen published models, most have one kind or leave lifetime unstated, and one rejects long-lived agents by design [13].
  5. Pre-approval and autonomy. An early version claimed a difference in its treatment of pre-approval and autonomy, and the claim is withdrawn. Charterion and AAM/5C state pre-approval more completely than WARM does.
