# Which source comes closest to a defined review of each human decision

**Cheng and Cheng (2026)** state the H3 purpose most directly. Their HITL component routes every approval, records whether a person or the system decided, and is meant to "identify patterns in human decisions, and adjust intervention criteria over time" (§III.E). It differs from a defined review in three ways: no process, role or cadence is specified; no record format is given beyond the API response; and no measure (approval rate, override rate) is defined. It is a design intent, not a mechanism.

**Scerri, Pynadath and Tambe (2002)** come closest to a working mechanism. In the first Electric Elves design, every decision produced a training example (attributes plus the user's choice), and the user was asked whether such decisions should be autonomous next time; a learned tree then set when to ask. That is a per-decision review that changed the rules. It differs in that the review was automatic (a classifier, not a person reviewing), it covered four decision types, and the authors dropped it because learned rules ignored team costs (for example, "A Friday delayed a meeting almost 50 times, each time by 5 minutes", §2.2).

**The AWS graduated-autonomy post (2026)** is the only source with an operating rule linking human decisions to authority: the operators' rolling rejection rate caps the safety score, and the score moves the agent between tiers that decide what needs human approval. It differs from a review in that nothing examines why people approved or rejected; the decisions are reduced to a rate.

# Further passages (protocol "also record")

Reducing people's decisions as a goal:
- Cheng and Cheng: "The design objective is to minimize unnecessary intervention while maintaining acceptable risk levels." §IV.A, p.5
- AHI SoK: "Core question: How can we reduce the human decision burden without sacrificing security guarantees?" §5.1
- UiPath: "With a human in the middle, you can reduce human involvement to validation, and then ML can reduce it even further." §3

Against reducing them, or warning about it:
- AHI SoK: "The path forward is not to remove humans from the loop but to make their participation more effective, efficient, and informed" (Conclusion); and "Always-allow memory permanently authorizes future instances of the same action and is the most common production strategy and the one with the worst long-term security profile, since each click monotonically expands the trusted surface." App. (fatigue mitigation)
- Approval to Abuse: "when context-dependent approvals are dynamically persisted as context-free syntactic rules, they enable attacks analogous to classical permission re-delegation" §Discussion. Agents elicited the target allow rules "in 58% of experimental instances" (Abstract).
- Observability Gap: "feedback that minimizes human workload (output-only evaluation) is insufficient to drive convergence" §4.
